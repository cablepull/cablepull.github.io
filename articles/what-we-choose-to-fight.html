What We Choose to Fight

On suffering, aggression, and the courage to break the cycle

cablepull · November 2026

Azim Khamisa & Ples Felix

Tariq Khamisa, 1974–1995

  • Tariq was a San Diego State student delivering pizza when he was shot on 21 January 1995.
  • Tony Hicks, 14, fired on the order of an 18-year-old fellow gang member during a robbery attempt. He was reported to be the youngest person in California tried as an adult for murder, and was sentenced to 25 years to life.
  • Azim, an investment banker, reached out to Ples Felix, Tony's grandfather and guardian, and the two co-founded the Tariq Khamisa Foundation that same year.
  • When Tony came up for parole in 2018, Azim spoke on his behalf. Released in 2019 after more than 24 years, Tony joined the foundation's board and spoke to students alongside Azim.
Tariq Khamisa Foundation →

In January 1995, a 20-year-old college student named Tariq Khamisa was delivering pizza in San Diego when a 14-year-old gang member named Tony Hicks shot and killed him.

Sit with that for a second. Twenty. Fourteen. A pizza order.

Tariq's father, Azim Khamisa, had every reason to hate that kid for the rest of his life. Nobody would have blamed him. Instead, he reached out to Ples Felix—Tony's grandfather and guardian—and the two of them started working together to keep other kids from ending up on either end of a gun.

Azim put it about as plainly as anyone could: there were victims at both ends of the gun.

I want to be clear about what that did and didn't do. It didn't bring Tariq back. It didn't make the murder any less evil. It didn't let Tony off the hook—he was tried as an adult and went to prison.

What it did was decide what happened next.

That's the part I keep coming back to.

Have you ever been hurt? I mean really hurt—betrayed, humiliated, blindsided by something you didn't deserve. On the job, at home, by somebody you trusted, or by plain bad luck. Most of us are carrying something we've had a hard time putting down, long after the thing itself was over.

We know suffering changes people. What we don't talk about much is how much say we actually have in which direction. Or how easily one person's pain becomes somebody else's.

So this is an essay about fighting. Not about giving up the fight. About aiming it.

The second arrow

The Sallatha Sutta

Saṃyutta Nikāya 36.6 · "The Arrow"

  • Part of the Pali Canon, the oldest surviving collection of the Buddha's teachings, preserved orally before being written down in Sri Lanka in the 1st century BCE.
  • The untaught person, hit by pain, grieves and laments over it, and so feels two pains, one bodily and one mental. The well-taught person feels only the first.
  • The sutta names the habit underneath: paṭighānusaya, which Thanissaro renders "resistance-obsession" and Bhikkhu Sujato "the underlying tendency for aversion."
Read the sutta →

About 2,500 years ago, the Buddha taught what's usually called the parable of the two arrows. In the Sallatha Sutta, a person gets hit with an arrow—and then gets hit with a second one, right after.

The first arrow is the pain. The second is what we do with it: the dwelling, the resisting, the story we keep telling ourselves about it.

"Just as if they were to shoot a man with an arrow and, right afterward, were to shoot him with another one, so that he would feel the pains of two arrows."

Sallatha Sutta, SN 36.6, trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu

The sutta even has a name for the habit underneath that second arrow: paṭighānusaya. Thanissaro Bhikkhu translates it as "resistance-obsession." I think of it as aversion you've practiced so many times it fires on its own.

"As he is touched by that painful feeling, he is resistant. Any resistance-obsession with regard to that painful feeling obsesses him."

Sallatha Sutta, SN 36.6, trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu

You've seen it. Somebody gets betrayed and starts distrusting people who never did a thing to them. Somebody gets humiliated and hears every bit of feedback as an attack. Somebody gets treated unjustly and gets so good at spotting injustice that they start finding it where there isn't any.

The original injury has become a lens. Everything after gets read through it.

Those of us in security have our own version of this. After a bad incident, there's a moment where the room decides what kind of team it's going to be. You can go hunting for someone to hang—and you'll find someone, you always do—and then watch everybody hide their mistakes for the next five years. Or you can run a blameless postmortem: hold the system accountable, fix the actual causes, and refuse to turn the pain of the breach into a fresh injury. Same incident. Very different second arrow.

It's easy to spot this in other people. It's a lot harder to spot in yourself—especially when your grievance is legit. And it usually is. That's exactly what makes it dangerous.

Marcus Aurelius

121–180 CE · Emperor 161–180

  • The last of Rome's "Five Good Emperors," and a lifelong student of Stoic philosophy.
  • Spent much of his reign at war on the Danube frontier against the Marcomanni and other tribes, and ruled through the Antonine Plague.
  • Wrote Meditations in Greek, around 170–180 CE, much of it on campaign during the Marcomannic Wars, as a private notebook. He never meant it to be published.
Read Meditations (Long trans.) →

Marcus Aurelius wrestled with the same thing almost two thousand years ago. This is a guy who ran the Roman Empire, commanded armies, and could have crushed pretty much anyone who crossed him. In his private notebook—the thing we now call Meditations, which he never meant for anyone else to read—he left himself this:

"The best way of avenging thyself is not to become like [the wrong-doer]."

Meditations 6.6, trans. George Long (the brackets are Long's)

Read that again. That's not a pacifist talking. That's a man with the power to do just about anything, reminding himself—over and over, by the look of it—that retaliation can turn into imitation. You set out to answer the injury and you end up copying it.

He was more worried about what the wrong would make him than about what the wrongdoer deserved.

Azim Khamisa landed in the same place, and he didn't have an empire. He had a dead son.

Accountability matters. I'm not arguing it doesn't. But accountability and retaliation are not the same thing. And when the urge to retaliate stops being understandable and starts being overwhelming, that difference is everything.

Don't become the thing that hurt you

Etty Hillesum

15 January 1914 – 30 November 1943

  • Born in Middelburg, Netherlands; studied law and Slavic languages in Amsterdam.
  • Kept a diary from 1941 to 1942, then wrote letters from Westerbork, the transit camp where Dutch Jews were held before the trains to Auschwitz and Sobibor.
  • Took a job with the Jewish Council in July 1942, then asked to be posted to its welfare department at Westerbork. Friends offered to hide her; she refused.
  • Deported on 7 September 1943 with her parents and her brother Mischa. None of them survived.
  • Her writing was published in 1981 as Het verstoorde leven (An Interrupted Life).
More on Etty Hillesum →

Etty Hillesum was a young Jewish woman in Amsterdam under Nazi occupation. She kept a diary. She took a job with the Jewish Council and then asked to be sent to Westerbork, the transit camp that was the last stop in the Netherlands before the trains east. Friends offered to hide her. She said no. She was deported in September 1943 and murdered at Auschwitz that November. She was 29.

In the middle of all of that, she wrote this:

"…each of us must turn inward and destroy in himself all that he thinks he ought to destroy in others. And remember that every atom of hate we add to this world makes it still more inhospitable."

Etty Hillesum, diary, 23 September 1942 · An Interrupted Life, trans. Arnold J. Pomerans

Destroy in yourself what you think you ought to destroy in others. Honestly, that's this whole essay in one line. She wrote it in September 1942, with the deportations already running. A few months later she wrote nearly the same thing again in a letter from Westerbork itself.

She wasn't excusing anything. She saw the brutality up close. What scared her was that hatred—even completely justified hatred—would just add fuel to the thing that was destroying everyone around her.

I want to be careful here. Nobody being persecuted owes anybody forgiveness, and nobody gets to turn Hillesum into a bar other victims are supposed to clear. I can call what she did courage without demanding it from anyone.

Here's where it gets complicated. Because I also believe in fighting back.

Frederick Douglass

c. 1818–1895

  • Born enslaved on Maryland's Eastern Shore; he never knew his exact birthday.
  • In 1833–34 he was hired out for a year to Edward Covey, a farmer known as a "slave-breaker." The fight came in August 1834, when Douglass was 16. (The 1845 Narrative gives 1833; historians date it to 1834.)
  • Escaped to freedom in 1838, and published his Narrative in 1845.
  • Became the most famous abolitionist orator in America, later a U.S. Marshal and Minister to Haiti.
Read the Narrative →

In August 1834, a sixteen-year-old enslaved kid named Frederick Douglass was hired out to Edward Covey, a man whose whole reputation was "breaking" enslaved people. Covey beat him for months. Then one morning, Douglass fought back. In his words, "We were at it for nearly two hours." Covey never laid a hand on him again.

"You have seen how a man was made a slave; you shall see how a slave was made a man."

Frederick Douglass, Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, 1845

He called it "the turning-point in my career as a slave." And ten years later, telling the story again, he was careful about what kind of fight it had been:

"I was strictly on the defensive, preventing him from injuring me, rather than trying to injure him."

Frederick Douglass, My Bondage and My Freedom, 1855

He wasn't out to hurt Covey. He was out to make him stop.

Nobody sane would tell Douglass he should've just been more patient.

So is that a contradiction? Hillesum refusing to hate, Douglass throwing hands?

I don't think so. The question was never fight or don't fight. The question is: what is the fight for?

Douglass used force to stop an injury that was happening to him, right then. Hillesum fought to keep what was being done to her from turning into hatred of an entire people. Marcus fought the urge to become the thing he condemned. Three completely different situations, and none of them boils down to a rule like "always forgive" or "always hit back."

What they share is that none of them let the injury write the whole response.

Aggression, aimed

This is where I think we get aggression wrong.

We talk about aggression like it's just hostility—something you point at a person. But there's something else in there too: the drive to confront what's intolerable, to not back down, to win when it's hard. That drive isn't evil. It's fuel.

It all depends on what you point it at.

You can aim it at humiliating the person who humiliated you—or at making damn sure you never do that to anybody else. You can use it to defend yourself against real mistreatment, or let it spill onto people whose only crime is reminding you of somebody. You can use it to feed a grievance, or to fight the grievance itself.

That last one takes a kind of courage nobody hands out medals for.

Retaliation feels good. People who share your anger will cheer you on. Not retaliating? There might be nothing. No apology. No vindication. No sign the person who did it even understands what they did. You may have to accept that an injustice happened without ever accepting that it was just.

And you have to do that without it turning into surrender.

When the fight changes the world

Ashoka

r. c. 268–232 BCE · Maurya Empire

  • Grandson of Chandragupta Maurya; ruled most of the Indian subcontinent from Pataliputra.
  • Conquered Kalinga, roughly today's Odisha, in about 261 BCE, eight years after his coronation.
  • Had edicts carved on rocks and pillars across the empire, the earliest readable written texts from India, setting out dhamma: restraint, tolerance and care for his subjects.
  • At Dhauli and Jaugada, in Kalinga itself, Rock Edicts XI–XIII, including the confession of remorse, are missing. Two special "Separate Kalinga Edicts" stand in their place.
The Edicts (Dhammika trans.) →

This isn't just personal. Whole societies make this choice.

Around 261 BCE, the Mauryan emperor Ashoka conquered Kalinga, on India's east coast. Then he did something conquerors almost never do: he carved the body count into stone himself and put it up across his empire.

"One hundred and fifty thousand were deported, one hundred thousand were killed and many more died (from other causes)."

Rock Edict XIII, trans. Ven. S. Dhammika

Don't get me wrong—he stayed an emperor. The very same edict warns the forest tribes that "despite his remorse" he still has "the power to punish them." He didn't give up his power. He changed what he was pointing it at.

Which raises the question. If you can conquer, is conquering really the best use of your strength? Or is the bigger win changing what your strength is for?

Leymah Gbowee

b. 1972 · Liberia

  • Was 17 when Liberia's first civil war broke out in 1989; trained as a trauma counselor and worked with former child soldiers.
  • Led the Women of Liberia Mass Action for Peace in 2003, bringing Christian women together with Muslim women organized by Asatu Bah Kenneth.
  • Charles Taylor resigned on 11 August 2003. The Comprehensive Peace Agreement was signed in Accra a week later, on 18 August.
  • Shared the 2011 Nobel Peace Prize with Ellen Johnson Sirleaf and Tawakkol Karman. Her story is told in the documentary Pray the Devil Back to Hell (2008).
Nobel Prize biography →

Leymah Gbowee had no empire and no army. She was a young mother in Liberia, a country chewed up by back-to-back civil wars, and she'd been working as a trauma counselor with former child soldiers. In 2003 she helped lead the Women of Liberia Mass Action for Peace: Christian and Muslim women together, dressed in white, sitting out in a field by the road Charles Taylor drove every day. They staged a sex strike. They would not go away.

When the peace talks in Accra, Ghana, dragged on while people back home kept dying, the women showed up there too. They sat down in the doorways of the negotiating hall, linked arms, and told the delegates nobody was leaving until there was a deal. When security moved to remove them, Gbowee threatened to strip, which in traditional belief puts a curse on the men who see it. The delegates stayed put. Within weeks Taylor was gone and a peace agreement was signed. In 2011 she shared the Nobel Peace Prize.

Nonviolent? Yes. Passive? Not even a little.

People mix up compassion with being soft, and nonviolence with being afraid of a fight. There is nothing soft about telling armed men they're not leaving the room. Ashoka had an army and reconsidered it. Gbowee had none and faced down the men who did.

We know how to recognize courage when someone charges an enemy. We're a lot worse at recognizing it when someone charges the conditions that make people enemies in the first place. That second charge might take more nerve.

When they won't let you help

There's one more fight, and it's a sneaky one.

Sometimes the person you're trying to pull out of it doesn't want to be pulled.

If you've ever tried to help a bitter friend, a struggling family member, or a hostile coworker, you know how this goes. You can see exactly how the resentment is wrecking them. You bring patience and real concern. And they hear criticism. Or they aren't ready to let go of something that's become part of who they are. Or you just aren't the person they can hear it from.

Daryl Davis

b. 1958 · Chicago

  • Rhythm and blues and boogie-woogie pianist who has toured with Chuck Berry and played with Jerry Lee Lewis.
  • In 1983, at a bar gig in Frederick, Maryland, a white man told him it was the first time he'd heard a Black man play as well as Jerry Lee Lewis. Over a drink, the man said he was in the Klan.
  • Has spent decades meeting Klan members. By his own count, about 200 have left. He keeps the robes some of them handed over, around 25 as of 2016.
  • Subject of the 2016 documentary Accidental Courtesy, which also shows activists who reject his approach.
daryldavis.com →

Daryl Davis is a Black blues pianist who's toured with Chuck Berry. He has spent decades sitting down, one on one, with members of the Ku Klux Klan. It started in 1983 with a man in a Maryland bar who couldn't believe a Black man played piano like Jerry Lee Lewis, and who turned out to be a Klansman. It kept going from there. Over the years a lot of them quit (he puts it at about 200), and some handed him their robes on the way out.

His approach is controversial, and the criticism is fair. A conversation doesn't dismantle organized racism, and nobody owes their safety to educating people who hate them. But listen to how he describes his part in it:

"I didn't convert anybody. They saw the light and converted themselves."

Daryl Davis, NPR, 2017

That's the part that matters here. He didn't make anybody change. He made room for them to.

When we try to help, it's easy to get so invested that their progress becomes our job and their refusal becomes our failure. The harder we push, the more frustrated we get. And eventually we're resenting the very person whose resentment we were trying to fix.

That's the second arrow again. Their hostility is the first hit. Our frustration and our wounded pride are the second. Now two people are suffering, and the "help" is making it worse.

There's something almost funny about getting angry at somebody because they can't stop being angry.

So check yourself. Am I still trying to help, or am I trying to be right? Do I care how they're doing, or do I just want them to admit I had a point? And when it's obviously not working—can I step back?

A person can be beyond your reach without being beyond help. Maybe your history makes you the wrong messenger. Maybe someone else gets through. Maybe life does. Stepping back isn't abandoning them, and compassion doesn't mean standing there taking abuse forever. Sometimes the responsible move is boundaries and distance.

The trick is doing that without turning their refusal into one more grievance. You don't get to decide they're hopeless just because you're out of moves. And you don't get to make their recovery the price of your own peace.

So what is bravery?

Go back to Azim Khamisa and Ples Felix.

The father of a murdered young man and the grandfather of the boy who killed him. When Tony came up for parole twenty-some years later, Azim spoke for him. When Tony got out, he joined them. They couldn't undo it. Neither one could walk away from it. But they could decide whether the rest of their lives would be ruled by that night, or whether something could be built out of it.

What they built didn't make Tariq's death okay. It didn't erase anybody's responsibility. It was a fight to keep the same thing from happening to somebody else's kid.

Line that up with the rest of them. Douglass making Covey stop. Hillesum refusing to let hate in—at Westerbork, of all places. Gbowee blocking a doorway in Accra. Marcus reminding himself, in his own notebook, not to become the thing he hated. Ashoka putting his regret in stone.

There's no single method there. Try to make one and you've missed the point. Sometimes the answer is confrontation. Sometimes it's restraint. Sometimes it's reaching for somebody who doesn't want to be reached. And sometimes it's admitting you can't do any more without making things worse.

What ties them together isn't giving up the fight. It's judgment about what's worth fighting.

We almost never call not retaliating "aggressive." We almost never call holding on to compassion "brave." We save those words for visible fights with a clear opponent and a winner.

But some of the hardest fights have no audience. The opponent is an impulse you'd really like to give in to. And winning gets you nothing except knowing that one injury didn't turn into another one.

The person who hurt you might never change. Your help might fail. You might even find out, years in, that you were feeding the very thing you were trying to stop. So the courage isn't just to keep going. It's the courage to look at your own conduct, admit when your methods aren't working, and accept your limits without letting disappointment curdle into bitterness.

None of this is easy, and none of these people are a template anybody is obligated to live up to. But they prove we have more options than revenge, surrender, or not caring.

We can oppose cruelty without being cruel. We can resist injustice without letting hate drive. We can use force when it's needed to stop harm, without pretending that hurting someone is an achievement. We can be compassionate toward people who make it really hard. And when we can't help, we can step aside without deciding they don't deserve a chance to change.

There's a kind of aggression I think is worth building: the stubborn, uncompromising refusal to let suffering keep moving through you.

Bring your fighting spirit to that. Not just against the people who wronged you—against the part of you that wants to wrong someone back.

You won't always dodge the first arrow. You won't always see the second one coming. But you can learn to feel the moment your pain starts turning into somebody else's.

And in that moment you might have every reason to keep fighting, every chance to hurt someone back—and still find the nerve to end it.

That is one of the bravest things a human being can do.
