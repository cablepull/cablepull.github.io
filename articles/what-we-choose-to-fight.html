What We Choose to Fight

On suffering, aggression, and the courage to break the cycle

cablepull · November 2026

Azim Khamisa & Ples Felix

Tariq Khamisa, 1974–1995

  • Tariq was a San Diego State student delivering pizza when he was shot on 21 January 1995.
  • Tony Hicks, 14, fired on the order of an 18-year-old fellow gang member during a robbery attempt. He was reported to be the youngest person in California tried as an adult for murder, and was sentenced to 25 years to life.
  • Azim, an investment banker, reached out to Ples Felix, Tony's grandfather and guardian, and the two co-founded the Tariq Khamisa Foundation that same year.
  • When Tony came up for parole in 2018, Azim spoke on his behalf. Released in 2019 after more than 24 years, Tony joined the foundation's board and spoke to students alongside Azim.
Tariq Khamisa Foundation →

In January 1995, a 20-year-old college student named Tariq Khamisa was delivering pizza in San Diego when a 14-year-old gang member named Tony Hicks shot and killed him.

I'm a father, and I can't read that without putting myself in it. Your son goes out to deliver a pizza. A pizza. He isn't a soldier shipping out. He isn't walking into a war zone. He's doing the kind of job nobody thinks twice about. And he doesn't come home.

I imagine it hitting all at once. Disbelief. Grief. Rage. The desperate need to find some reason it makes sense. Except there isn't one. One of the most important people in your life is gone because he went out to bring somebody dinner. Nothing about that will ever make sense.

Tariq's father, Azim Khamisa, was living that. In the middle of it—not years later, with time and distance to soften it, but while he was still grieving his son—he saw something most of us never would. He looked at the fourteen-year-old who pulled the trigger and saw another victim.

"There were victims at both ends of the gun."

Azim Khamisa

That takes a clarity few of us would have in that moment. Tariq was a victim of that gang. So was Tony: a fourteen-year-old handed a gun and told to shoot by an eighteen-year-old who should have known better. That doesn't excuse what Tony did. It means that when everything in Azim had every reason to see only one piece of the picture, he saw the whole thing.

He acted on it. He reached out to Ples Felix—Tony's grandfather and guardian—and the two of them started working together to keep other kids from ending up on either end of a gun.

I want to be clear about what that did and didn't do. It didn't bring Tariq back. It didn't make the murder any less evil. It didn't let Tony off the hook.

What it did was decide what happened next.

Most of us have been hurt in a way we didn't deserve: betrayed, humiliated, blindsided. On the job, at home, by someone we trusted, or by plain bad luck. Many of us carry something we've had a hard time putting down, long after the thing itself was over.

We know suffering changes people. What we don't talk about much is how much say we actually have in which direction. Or how easily one person's pain becomes somebody else's.

This essay is about fighting, and about where we aim it.

Suffering doesn't stay put. It moves from person to person, and right now it's everywhere. Every one of us is a place where it either keeps going or stops.

The second arrow

The Sallatha Sutta

Saṃyutta Nikāya 36.6 · "The Arrow"

  • Part of the Pali Canon, the oldest surviving collection of the Buddha's teachings, preserved orally before being written down in Sri Lanka in the 1st century BCE.
  • The untaught person, hit by pain, grieves and laments over it, and so feels two pains, one bodily and one mental. The well-taught person feels only the first.
  • The sutta names the habit underneath: paṭighānusaya, which Thanissaro renders "resistance-obsession" and Bhikkhu Sujato "the underlying tendency for aversion."
Read the sutta →

The Buddha told of a man struck by an arrow, then struck by a second one right after.

The first arrow is the pain. The second is what we do with it: the dwelling, the resisting, the story we keep telling ourselves about it.

"Just as if they were to shoot a man with an arrow and, right afterward, were to shoot him with another one, so that he would feel the pains of two arrows."

Sallatha Sutta, SN 36.6, trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu

The sutta even has a name for the habit underneath that second arrow: paṭighānusaya. Thanissaro Bhikkhu translates it as "resistance-obsession." I think of it as aversion you've practiced so many times it fires on its own.

"As he is touched by that painful feeling, he is resistant. Any resistance-obsession with regard to that painful feeling obsesses him."

Sallatha Sutta, SN 36.6, trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu

Somebody gets betrayed and starts distrusting people who never did a thing to them. A humiliated person hears every bit of feedback as an attack. Somebody gets treated unjustly and gets so good at spotting injustice that they start finding it where there isn't any.

The original injury isn't just a memory anymore. It's a fractured perspective, and everything that comes after gets viewed through that one broken facet.

You lose something there. A person who could once take in the whole picture—the context, the other person's side, the chance that it wasn't about them at all—just can't anymore. The grief and the damage narrow everything down to a point. All of your attention gets focused on the place that hurts.

I don't think that's a defect. It's a reaction we need. When you get hurt, narrowing in on the injury is how you survive it. It's the same reflex that yanks your hand off the stove. But it's an emergency mode. It was never meant to be a place you move into and live. Stay there long enough and it stops protecting you and starts running you.

It's easy to spot this in other people and much harder to spot in yourself, especially when the grievance is legitimate. It usually is, and that is what makes it dangerous.

Marcus Aurelius

121–180 CE · Emperor 161–180

  • The last of Rome's "Five Good Emperors," and a lifelong student of Stoic philosophy.
  • Spent much of his reign at war on the Danube frontier against the Marcomanni and other tribes, and ruled through the Antonine Plague.
  • Wrote Meditations in Greek, around 170–180 CE, much of it on campaign during the Marcomannic Wars, as a private notebook. He never meant it to be published.
Read Meditations (Long trans.) →

Marcus Aurelius wrestled with the same thing almost two thousand years ago, and left himself this note:

"The best way of avenging thyself is not to become like [the wrong-doer]."

Meditations 6.6, trans. George Long (the brackets are Long's)

This came from a man who could have answered almost any wrong with the full weight of Rome. He wrote it to himself, in a notebook nobody was meant to read, and the idea keeps coming back throughout Meditations. He understood that retaliation has a way of turning into imitation.

He was more worried about what the wrong would make him than about what the wrongdoer deserved.

His son Commodus inherited the empire, but not the notebook. He ruled through suspicion and spectacle, and in 192 CE he was strangled in his bath.

Azim Khamisa reached the same conclusion as Marcus, and he didn't have an empire. He had a dead son.

Accountability matters. I'm not arguing it doesn't. But accountability and retaliation are not the same thing. When the urge to retaliate stops being understandable and starts being overwhelming, that difference is everything.

Don't become the thing that hurt you

Etty Hillesum

15 January 1914 – 30 November 1943

  • Born in Middelburg, Netherlands; studied law and Slavic languages in Amsterdam.
  • Kept a diary from 1941 to 1942, then wrote letters from Westerbork, the transit camp where Dutch Jews were held before the trains to Auschwitz and Sobibor.
  • Took a job with the Jewish Council in July 1942, then asked to be posted to its welfare department at Westerbork. Friends offered to hide her; she refused.
  • Deported on 7 September 1943 with her parents and her brother Mischa. None of them survived.
  • Her writing was published in 1981 as Het verstoorde leven (An Interrupted Life).
More on Etty Hillesum →

Etty Hillesum had every reason to hate, and she spent the last years of her life refusing to. In September 1942, with the deportations already running, she wrote this in her diary:

"…each of us must turn inward and destroy in himself all that he thinks he ought to destroy in others. And remember that every atom of hate we add to this world makes it still more inhospitable."

Etty Hillesum, diary, 23 September 1942 · An Interrupted Life, trans. Arnold J. Pomerans

Destroy in yourself what you think you ought to destroy in others. A few months later she wrote nearly the same thing again from Westerbork, the camp she had asked to be sent to.

She wasn't excusing anything. She saw the brutality up close. What scared her was that hatred—even completely justified hatred—would just add fuel to the thing that was destroying everyone around her.

I want to be careful here. Nobody being persecuted owes anybody forgiveness, and nobody gets to turn Hillesum into a bar other victims are supposed to clear. I can call what she did courage without demanding it from anyone.

I also believe in fighting back.

Frederick Douglass

c. 1818–1895

  • Born enslaved on Maryland's Eastern Shore; he never knew his exact birthday.
  • In 1833–34 he was hired out for a year to Edward Covey, a farmer known as a "slave-breaker." The fight came in August 1834, when Douglass was 16. (The 1845 Narrative gives 1833; historians date it to 1834.)
  • Escaped to freedom in 1838, and published his Narrative in 1845.
  • Became the most famous abolitionist orator in America, later a U.S. Marshal and Minister to Haiti.
Read the Narrative →

At sixteen, Frederick Douglass fought back. Edward Covey, the "slave-breaker" he had been hired out to, had beaten him for months. In his words, "We were at it for nearly two hours." Covey never laid a hand on him again.

"You have seen how a man was made a slave; you shall see how a slave was made a man."

Frederick Douglass, Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, 1845

He called it "the turning-point in my career as a slave." Ten years later, telling the story again, he was careful about what kind of fight it had been:

"I was strictly on the defensive, preventing him from injuring me, rather than trying to injure him."

Frederick Douglass, My Bondage and My Freedom, 1855

No one would tell Douglass he should have been more patient.

Hillesum refusing to hate and Douglass fighting back can look like a contradiction.

They aren't. What separates them is what each fight was for.

Douglass used force to stop an injury that was happening to him, right then. Hillesum fought to keep what was being done to her from turning into hatred of an entire people. Marcus fought the urge to become the thing he condemned. Three completely different situations, and none of them boils down to a rule like "always forgive" or "always hit back."

What they share is that none of them let the injury write the whole response.

Aggression, aimed

Aggression usually gets described as hostility, something pointed at a person. It is also the drive to confront what's intolerable, to hold your ground, to keep going when it's hard. That drive is fuel, and fuel doesn't care what it burns.

It all depends on what you point it at.

You can aim it at humiliating the person who humiliated you—or at making damn sure you never do that to anybody else. You can use it to defend yourself against real mistreatment, or let it spill onto people whose only crime is reminding you of somebody. You can use it to feed a grievance, or to fight the grievance itself.

That last fight is the one nobody praises.

Retaliation feels good. People who share your anger will cheer you on. Refusing to retaliate might bring you nothing. No apology. No vindication. No sign the person who did it even understands what they did. You may have to make peace with the fact that it happened, without ever agreeing that it was okay.

That acceptance can't become surrender.

When the fight changes the world

Ashoka

r. c. 268–232 BCE · Maurya Empire

  • Grandson of Chandragupta Maurya; ruled most of the Indian subcontinent from Pataliputra.
  • Conquered Kalinga, roughly today's Odisha, in about 261 BCE, eight years after his coronation, and kept it.
  • Had edicts carved on rocks and pillars across the empire, the earliest readable written texts from India, setting out dhamma: restraint, tolerance and care for his subjects.
  • At Dhauli and Jaugada, in Kalinga itself, Rock Edicts XI–XIII, including the confession of remorse, are missing. Two special "Separate Kalinga Edicts" stand in their place.
The Edicts (Dhammika trans.) →

Whole societies face this choice too.

After conquering Kalinga, the emperor Ashoka did something conquerors almost never do: he carved the body count into stone himself and put it up across his empire.

"One hundred and fifty thousand were deported, one hundred thousand were killed and many more died (from other causes)."

Rock Edict XIII, trans. Ven. S. Dhammika

His regret was real enough to carve into stone. But he kept Kalinga. The same edict warns the forest peoples within his empire that "despite his remorse" he still has "the power to punish them." The confession was never carved in Kalinga itself. Remorse without repair is still the first arrow, just with an apology attached.

Regret can open the door to breaking a cycle. Ashoka stood in that doorway and kept the house.

Leymah Gbowee

b. 1972 · Liberia

  • Was 17 when Liberia's first civil war broke out in 1989; trained as a trauma counselor and worked with former child soldiers.
  • Led the Women of Liberia Mass Action for Peace in 2003, bringing Christian women together with Muslim women organized by Asatu Bah Kenneth.
  • Charles Taylor resigned on 11 August 2003. The Comprehensive Peace Agreement was signed in Accra a week later, on 18 August.
  • Shared the 2011 Nobel Peace Prize with Ellen Johnson Sirleaf and Tawakkol Karman. Her story is told in the documentary Pray the Devil Back to Hell (2008).
Nobel Prize biography →

Leymah Gbowee had no empire and no army. She had a country chewed up by civil war and a movement of Christian and Muslim women dressed in white, sitting in a field by the road Charles Taylor drove every day. They announced they would refuse sex with their husbands until there was peace. Gbowee later admitted it did little in practice, but it got the world's attention. They would not go away.

When the peace talks in Accra, Ghana, dragged on while people back home kept dying, the women showed up there too. They sat down in the doorways of the negotiating hall, linked arms, and told the delegates nobody was leaving until there was a deal. When security moved to remove them, Gbowee threatened to strip, which in traditional belief puts a curse on the men who see it. The delegates stayed put. Within weeks Taylor was gone and a peace agreement was signed.

Their weapon was refusing to leave.

People mix up compassion with being soft, and nonviolence with being afraid of a fight. There is nothing soft about telling armed men they're not leaving the room. Ashoka had an army and regretted using it. Gbowee had none and faced down the men who did.

We know how to recognize courage when someone charges an enemy. We're a lot worse at recognizing it when someone charges the conditions that make people enemies in the first place. That second charge might take more nerve.

When they won't let you help

One more fight is easy to miss.

Sometimes the person you're trying to pull out of it doesn't want to be pulled.

If you've ever tried to help a bitter friend, a struggling family member, or a hostile coworker, you know how this goes. You can see exactly how the resentment is wrecking them. You bring patience and real concern. They hear criticism. Or they aren't ready to let go of something that's become part of who they are. Or you just aren't the person they can hear it from.

Daryl Davis

b. 1958 · Chicago

  • Rhythm and blues and boogie-woogie pianist who has toured with Chuck Berry and played with Jerry Lee Lewis.
  • In 1983, at a bar gig in Frederick, Maryland, a white man told him it was the first time he'd heard a Black man play as well as Jerry Lee Lewis. Over a drink, the man said he was in the Klan.
  • Has spent decades meeting Klan members. By his own count, about 200 have left. He keeps the robes some of them handed over, around 25 as of 2016.
  • Subject of the 2016 documentary Accidental Courtesy, which also shows activists who reject his approach.
daryldavis.com →

Daryl Davis, a Black musician, has spent decades sitting down one on one with members of the Ku Klux Klan. Over the years a lot of them quit (he puts it at about 200), and some handed him their robes on the way out.

The robes are what people remember. I think about what isn't in that closet. Two hundred people across decades means far more conversations that went nowhere: men who listened politely and changed nothing, men who never stopped hating him. Davis had to accept that kind of failure again and again, and keep it from hardening into a hatred of his own.

His approach is controversial, and the criticism is fair. A conversation doesn't dismantle organized racism, and nobody owes their safety to educating people who hate them. Still, his own account of his part is worth hearing:

"I didn't convert anybody. They saw the light and converted themselves."

Daryl Davis, NPR, 2017

He didn't make anybody change. He made room for them to.

When we try to help, it's easy to get so invested that their progress becomes our job and their refusal becomes our failure. The harder we push, the more frustrated we get. Eventually we're resenting the very person whose resentment we were trying to fix.

That's the second arrow again. Their hostility is the first hit. Our frustration and our wounded pride are the second. Now two people are suffering, and the "help" is making it worse.

There is a bitter irony in getting angry at someone because they can't stop being angry.

A few honest questions help. Am I still trying to help, or am I trying to be right? Do I care how they're doing, or do I just want them to admit I had a point? When it's obviously not working—can I step back?

A person can be beyond your reach without being beyond help. Maybe your history makes you the wrong messenger. Maybe someone else gets through. Maybe life does. Compassion was never supposed to mean standing there taking abuse forever, and stepping back isn't the same as giving up on someone.

The hard part is doing that without turning their refusal into one more grievance, and that takes a little humility. It takes some ego to believe we're the right messenger, or that we're the one who's going to fix them. They have their own agency and their own way back, and it may not run through us. You don't get to decide they're hopeless just because you're out of moves. You don't get to make their recovery the price of your own peace.

So what is bravery?

Return to Azim Khamisa and Ples Felix.

The father of a murdered young man and the grandfather of the boy who killed him. When Tony came up for parole twenty-some years later, Azim spoke for him. When Tony got out, he joined them. They couldn't undo it. Neither one could walk away from it. But they could decide whether the rest of their lives would be ruled by that night, or whether something could be built out of it.

What they built didn't make Tariq's death okay. It didn't erase anybody's responsibility. It was a fight to keep the same thing from happening to somebody else's kid.

Set that beside the others. Douglass making Covey stop. Hillesum refusing to let hate in—at Westerbork, of all places. Gbowee blocking a doorway in Accra. Marcus reminding himself, in his own notebook, not to become the thing he hated.

There's no single method there. Forcing one would miss the point. Sometimes the answer is confrontation. Sometimes it's restraint. Sometimes it's reaching for somebody who doesn't want to be reached. Sometimes it's admitting you can't do any more without making things worse.

What ties them together is judgment about what's worth fighting.

We almost never call not retaliating "aggressive." We almost never call holding on to compassion "brave." We save those words for visible fights with a clear opponent and a winner.

But some of the hardest fights have no audience. The opponent is an impulse you'd really like to give in to. Winning gets you nothing except knowing that one injury didn't turn into another one.

The person who hurt you might never change. Your help might fail. You might even find out, years in, that you were feeding the very thing you were trying to stop. Courage here means more than persistence. It means looking at your own conduct, admitting when your methods aren't working, and accepting your limits without letting disappointment curdle into bitterness.

None of this is easy, and none of these people are a template anybody is obligated to live up to. But they prove we have more options than revenge, surrender, or not caring.

We can oppose cruelty without being cruel. We can resist injustice without letting hate drive. We can use force when it's needed to stop harm, without pretending that hurting someone is an achievement. We can be compassionate toward people who make it really hard. When we can't help, we can step aside without deciding they don't deserve a chance to change.

There's a kind of aggression I think is worth building: the stubborn, uncompromising refusal to let suffering keep moving through you.

Bring your fighting spirit to that. Not just against the people who wronged you—against the part of you that wants to wrong someone back.

You won't always dodge the first arrow. You won't always see the second one coming. But you can learn to feel the moment your pain starts turning into somebody else's.

In that moment you might have every reason to keep fighting, every chance to hurt someone back—and still find the nerve to end it.

That is one of the bravest things a human being can do.
