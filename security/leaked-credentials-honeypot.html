The life of a leaked AWS key

For four months, a server pretended to be a careless developer's box. It left out .git folders, .env files, SMTP logins, AI API endpoints and hundreds of AWS keys that reported back the moment someone used them. This page walks through what happened to those credentials.

The research and every figure on this page come from Lauren Proehl, “I leaked credentials on purpose for four months” (THOR Collective Dispatch, 12 Nov 2026). The charts, diagrams, animations and commentary here are mine, built from her published numbers. None are taken from her post. Read the original for her full method, crew details and hunt queries.

01The trap

Every lure was safe by construction. The .git directory could be walked and rebuilt into a repo full of secrets. The SMTP server accepted any login and any message, then delivered nothing. The fake OpenAI, Anthropic, Azure, Vertex and DeepSeek endpoints accepted any key. Each AWS key was unique to the scanner that took it and had no permissions, so every call failed but still raised an alert inside AWS.

That per-scanner uniqueness is the clever part. When a key shows up somewhere, you know exactly who took it and when, which lets you measure the gap between theft and use.

02The clock starts immediately

About one in three stolen keys was ever used, and that rate held steady for all four months. For the keys that were used, the gap was short.

0 minmedian theft → first use
  • 1m 06sThe fastest key went from stolen to used in about a minute.
  • ≤ 5 min36% of used keys were tried within five minutes, faster than most rotation runbooks.
  • ~37 minMedian time to first use (n = 103 keys with a known theft time).
  • > 1 dayAbout 1 in 13 sat for more than a day before anyone used it.
  • 37.6 daysThe slowest key was used more than five weeks after it was taken.

What this means: you have minutes to rotate and weeks to keep watching. A key that's still quiet after a day hasn't been forgotten. It's sitting in someone's stockpile.

The first question attackers ask isn't “what data can I reach?” It's “can I send mail?” A working send path is worth more to spam and phishing operations than most of what sits in a bucket.

LLMjacking moves in · share of newly used keys that tried Bedrock, by month first used

The jump came from roughly 20 unrelated networks, so it reflects a market shift rather than one crew. Stolen cloud keys are now also stolen AI compute.

The grader

On October 9 a client calling itself harvest-check/1.0 hit the fake AI endpoints with 20 requests in under five minutes. They came through residential connections in 14 countries, used cheap models, capped replies at 2–20 tokens, and included prompts meant to tell a real endpoint from a fake one. Stolen AI keys are checked for quality before they're resold.

The SMTP bait drew 18,701 sessions from 2,188 IPs. 103 IPs logged in with the planted credentials, and 205 IPs submitted message bodies. Nothing was delivered.

Also seen: 419 advance-fee scams, a Portuguese account-takeover lure and plain relay tests.

The most useful finding was a credential checker exposing its own pipeline. It tested stolen SMTP logins, then emailed its operator through the fake server to report the ones that worked. That happened 159 times from 12 IPs, with subject lines shaped like this:

valid host|port|user|password

About a quarter of everyone who submitted a message wasn't phishing. They were testing the relay or reporting working credentials. A stolen SMTP login is mostly an input to someone else's spam business.

08What to do about it

PRIORITY

Treat messaging creds as crown jewels

SES, SNS and SMTP credentials get tested first and used as soon as they work. Scope them tightly and alert on any new sender.

TIME

Rotate in minutes, watch for weeks

A third of keys were used within five minutes, and about 1 in 13 more than a day later. Revocation has to be automated, and monitoring has to keep going after it.

STRATEGY

Revoke the credential, not the IP

Thieves and users sit on different networks, and reputation feeds miss most users. Detect the exposure and kill the key.

COVERAGE

Log Bedrock properly

Confirm CloudTrail captures Bedrock control-plane calls, and turn on data events for InvokeModel and Converse. Otherwise LLMjacking doesn't show up in your logs.

HARDEN

Enforce IMDSv2

Nearly 2,000 SSRF attempts went after instance metadata. IMDSv2 blocks the simple version of that attack.

DECEPTION

Plant canaries

Nearly everything on this page came from fake credentials that report back. They're cheap, quiet and accurate. Put them where real secrets leak.
